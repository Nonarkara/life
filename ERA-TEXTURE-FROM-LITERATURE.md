## Rules for using them

1. **Fiction gives texture and plausibility, never a dated fact.** A date, price or law needs a historical source (era plan, section 5). The novel's prices (for example a wartime ampoule costing thousands of baht) are story, not data.
2. **No reproduction.** Kukrit died in 1995, so the books are in copyright. All game text is original; nothing is quoted or closely paraphrased.
3. **Novel characters are not cameos.** Cameos stay deceased public figures with a sourced row (plan, section 9).
4. **The funeral book's subject is a private person.** She is not put in the game. The book is a source for the *form* only: a Thai life summed up in a short biography, a tribute and a sermon, which is a better ending than a score.

## Components of life the books add to an era pack

Each is a data slot in the pack format, not new code.

- **Household as the unit of fate.** A principal wife, lesser wives, and children whose standing follows the mother. A child may be sent to serve in a great household or the palace. This is the plan's "structural inequality as mechanic" in concrete form: your odds depend on whose child you are within the house, not just which house.
- **Rank and address.** Titles change with reigns and promotions, and people are re-named by them (khun, luang, phra, phraya; a rank often changes how everyone addresses you). Rank is a meter that moves through events (coronations, honours, retirement).
- **Water, then road, then rail.** Riverside house with a landing and a wall (1880s to 1900s); first motor car and a stable closed down (1910s to 20s); a student returning by ship to Singapore or Penang, then train to a Bangkok station (1920s to 30s). Transport is the era's best prop and sound.
- **Dress as a clock.** The wrapped lower cloth gives way to skirts and western coats; hair, hats and perfume follow. Wardrobe sets per era (plan, section 4.3) can be driven by this order.
- **Palace and court as a separate life.** Mothers in service, orders and ranks, the death of a king ending a way of life for many households at once (the 1910 and 1925 changeovers, the 1932 change).
- **The returning student.** The Thai who comes back from abroad and sees home afresh: climate that feeds you, land that does not freeze, against crowded rented rooms and winter abroad. A strong event type for 1920s to 60s births.
- **Illness and scarcity.** Chronic illness, a family nursing, and the war years when medicine is rare, prices climb and black-market sellers appear. The doctor's limit and the sick person's calm about it. Maps to the health meter and to wartime inflation as a real constraint.
- **Karma as the household's reasoning.** People explain events by merit and consequence, in the open and without preaching. Never mocked, never promoted (Mantra 6).
- **Radio as the public voice (1960s).** A nightly programme explaining the world to ordinary Thais at the height of the Cold War. A cameo-style "witnessed" scene with no scripted words: the family listens.
- **Pets and servants as witnesses.** The dog story is a household seen from below, which is a fair test of whether our recap can show a life from someone other than its owner.
- **Death rites as a system.** Merit-making, ordination, building a gate or hall at a temple in someone's name, a funeral book with a short life summary. These are also late-life choices that cost money and shape a legacy.
